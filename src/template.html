What I wanted to know

What if just one car in 22 drove itself?

In my runs, one smoothing AI car took the speed swing from 8.8 mph down to 1.2 mph, and hard braking disappeared. The real test points the same way. In 2016, researchers drove a loop just like this one in Tucson with a single automated car and cut the speed swing by 50 to 81 percent and fuel use by 22 to 43 percent.

The surprises came at the extremes. One copycat cruise-control car made the jam a little worse. And when all 22 cars ran the smoothing controller, traffic went smooth but slow, settling around 9.7 mph, because each car only copies its own past speed and nobody is left to set the pace.

Why does a jam move backward?

Physicists model traffic like a fluid. A jam is a wave: cars leave its front and new cars join its back, so the wave rolls upstream while every car keeps moving forward. In a 2008 experiment run by Japanese physicists, 22 drivers on a 230 m track were asked to cruise at about 30 km/h. A jam formed with no bottleneck at all and rolled backward at roughly 20 km/h (12 mph), about the speed jams move on real highways.

MIT mathematicians later called these waves jamitons and found their equations look a lot like the ones for detonation waves from explosions. They even have a point that works like a black hole's event horizon: news that the road ahead is clear cannot reach the drivers stuck behind it.

Could AI warn about a jam, or fix one, on the Van Wyck?

Fixing one takes better software than most cars have today. Researchers tested seven 2018 car models with adaptive cruise control, and all seven made small slowdowns bigger. In a line of seven identical cars, a 6 mph dip grew to 25 mph by the back. The copycat setting here uses response numbers fitted to one of those cars. Smoothing does work, but it needs a buffer gap, and on a crowded expressway someone will cut into it. The 100-car test on I-24 near Nashville in November 2022 had to tune its cars so the gaps they left would not annoy or invite other drivers.

Warning about one is harder than I expected. The risk gauge above comes from a model I trained on 2,500 simulated runs to predict a jam 60 seconds ahead. On runs it never saw, it scored 0.82 on a measure called AUC (0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect), beating a simple rule of thumb at 0.70. At its alert setting it caught about two thirds of jams, but only about half its alerts came true. Phantom jams grow out of tiny random wobbles, which is exactly what makes them hard to see coming.

What this sim leaves out

One lane, no exits, no lane changes, no trucks. The human drivers follow the Intelligent Driver Model, a standard textbook model, plus a little random noise. The smoothing AI only knows its own speed and the gap ahead. My speed gains come out bigger than the real experiment's, likely because my simulated drivers stop completely more often than the real ones did.
